# Summary
These open educational resources offer hands-on instruction in crafting compelling digital experiences which enable users to interactively engage with computation and data. Originally taught at the University of California, Berkeley in 2025 [@Catalogue] and in a number of guest lectures and workshops across multiple schools and programs [@PottingerTeaching], these labs and lectures provide a multi-disciplinary approach. Specifically, these open source materials begin with traditional information design [@Munzner] and perception science [@Ware]. Then, instruction extends to meet emerging needs through broad perspectives including media studies [@Hall], human-computer interaction [@Harrison], user research [@Shneiderman], and game design [@Schell]. Altogether, through applied skills labs and guided practical projects which explore required software engineering, this course offers scientists, engineers, journalists, and others an opportunity to critically consider the role of the designers / developers constructing interfaces to information and to act on the unique possibilities of digital media [@VictorDead], including inviting audiences to co-create meaning [@Portnow]. Historically situated [@Plato] but embracing modern tools such as AI, these re-usable resources invite instructors and learners to foster rooted practices in the creation of "media for thinking the unthinkable" [@VictorMedia], supporting modern scientific exploration and storytelling through computation.

# Statement of Need
Existing open data visualization instruction often takes a perception science [@StahmerReynolds] and user-centered design approach [@MunznerYoutube] but may lean more on using charting systems or static drawing than fully custom software [@Cario]. Therefore, some valued resources choose not to center creating new bespoke experiences possible with custom programming [@MarcusWu]. In response to current open offerings and experience lecturing within existing curricula [@PottingerTeaching], these educational materials instead assume some programming capability which enables the creation of new custom interactive graphical forms, honoring commonly-taught classical principles [@ClevelandMcGill] but freeing them to be used as ingredients and not as prescriptive recipes [@PottingerTools]. Rooted in the historic practice of exploring new visual forms [@Tufte], this reaches beyond the standard list of charts towards giving students the capacity to build pieces similar to the custom interactive experiences which often light up the classroom but which also often remain out of reach for students to actually construct [@Harris; @Rees; @PottingerAfscgap]. This embrace of software engineering to augment information design enables teachers and learners to update traditional data visualization instruction to support applications in science-informed policymaking [@Enroads; @PottingerPlasticsTool; @PottingerPlasticsPaper] and interactive science [@VictorExplorable], meeting today’s opportunities created by computers' ever-growing capacity to react to the user [@VictorDead]. In total, these resources seek to empower data professionals to blend evidence-based design with the expressive capabilities of development.

# Objective, Design, and Content
Through hands-on projects, these open source educational materials aim to both empower students to critically interrogate and design digital media as well as the capability to build rich bespoke interactive data experiences. In addition to discussing ethics and accessibility, a multi-part course design offers different "perspectives" on data visualization as:

 - *Representation*: exploring a traditional perception science approach to information design [@Ware].
 - *Task*: bringing students modern human-centered design conceptions of data visualization [@Munzner].
 - *Message*: branching into sociology, anthropology, and media studies [@DiagrammaticCovid; @Hall].
 - *Dialogue*: unlocking the unique capabilities of digital interactivity [@VictorDead] that explore audiences as co-creators of meaning, including through game design [@Portnow].

This mix of traditional lecture content with hands-on labs and projects^[Each entry includes its own citations which may extend beyond those listed here.] also often include supplementary captioned video and PDFs. Altogether, these offer the experience of programming to not just learn interactive information design but to feel and understand the role of human iteration. Finally, the course concludes with a critical but applied consideration of AI in visualization construction.

# Use
Due to a blended design and engineering instruction, these materials are compiled from markdown and YAML to HTML at https://mooc.interactivedatascience.courses/ via custom Python as described in the repository. All materials available under a Creative Commons license with code as open source. Using modern technologies like WebAssembly, one may complete all instruction without installing local software beyond a modern web browser.

# Background
These materials were taught as a full course^[Original offering was for 2 credit hours upper-division undergraduate or graduate. Use of some optional materials may allow for use in a 3 credit hour setting.] at the University of California, Berkeley in 2025 [@Catalogue]. Additionally, these materials have also served different programs and universities as components in other instruction or as workshops [@PottingerTeaching]. Individual lessons may provide topic-specific supplement to other instruction, modules may offer a treatment of a topical area for learning, and the class as a whole may allow for an approximate semester of hybrid hands-on and lecture-based exploration.
